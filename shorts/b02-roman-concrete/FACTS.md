| Romans mixed quicklime directly in ("hot mixing"), which leaves white lime lumps (lime clasts) in the concrete | [V] | Seymour, Masic et al., "Hot mixing: Mechanistic insights into the durability of ancient Roman concrete", *Science Advances* 9 (2023), doi:10.1126/sciadv.add1602. | Cracks run into the lumps; water dissolves the lime, which recrystallises as calcium carbonate and fills the crack | [V] | MIT News; Scientific American. |
| 2023: lab samples made with the hot-mixed recipe were cracked and water was run through; within two weeks the cracks sealed and water no longer flowed | [V], see note | Reported by MIT-based coverage (my9nj/CBS, ScienceAlert) and Engineerine (14 days). I could not open the paper (403) or the MIT page in this session, so read the paper's results section once before publishing. |
| The same mix without quicklime never healed; water kept flowing | [V] | Scientific American; MIT coverage. |
| v2 line 1: quicklime in a wet mix "began to boil" | [V] | Slaking quicklime is strongly exothermic; the study's point is exactly this heat ("hot mixing"). The boiling picture is a fair visual, not a measured temperature. |
| v2 line 9: each lump is lime that never got wet | [V] | MIT coverage: hot mixing leaves lime clasts of unreacted, reactive calcium that water later dissolves. |
| v2 line 12: "MIT scientists" | [V] | MIT-led team with Harvard and labs in Italy and Switzerland. |
| v2 line 15: Roman concrete "has had two thousand years" | [V] | Roman concrete structures (harbours, walls) are roughly 2,000 years old. Said about Roman concrete in general, not about the test. Ending visual = Roman harbour ruins, which use a different (seawater) chemistry, so the line must not say they healed by this mechanism. |
| Pantheon (v1 only, cut in v2) | removed | Cut because placing it after the test implied the Pantheon heals itself. |
